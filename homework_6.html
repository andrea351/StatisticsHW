Homework 6 – Contingency tables and the chi-square test of independence

Given any bivariate dataset (X, F), programmatically construct the contingency table and calculate conditional and marginal frequencies. Compute the expected frequencies under the assumption of independence and the chi-square statistic (optionally the p-value). Recall the theoretical concepts of independence in statistics and probability, interpret the results to decide whether the two variables are independent or dependent, and discuss the conclusions, distinguishing statistical association from causality.

Independence: the theory

In probability

Two events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent when \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)\). If \(P(A) > 0\) this is the same as \(P(B \mid A) = P(B)\): knowing that \(A\) happened does not change the probability of \(B\).

Two discrete random variables \(X\) and \(F\), with values \(x_1,\dots,x_r\) and \(f_1,\dots,f_c\), are independent when every pair of events \(\{X = x_i\}\) and \(\{F = f_j\}\) is independent:

\[ p_{ij} = P(X = x_i,\ F = f_j) = P(X = x_i)\,P(F = f_j) = p_{i\cdot}\,p_{\cdot j} \qquad \text{for all } i, j . \]

Equivalently, every conditional distribution equals the marginal one: \(P(F = f_j \mid X = x_i) = p_{\cdot j}\) for every \(i\). The joint distribution then carries no information beyond its two marginals. Independence is a property of the whole joint distribution, not of single cells; and for numerical variables it is stronger than zero correlation, which only rules out a linear relation.

In a sample

On \(n\) observed pairs the probabilities are replaced by frequencies. With \(n_{ij}\) the number of pairs \((x_i, f_j)\) and the marginal totals \(n_{i\cdot} = \sum_j n_{ij}\), \(n_{\cdot j} = \sum_i n_{ij}\):

\[ \text{joint } \frac{n_{ij}}{n}, \qquad \text{marginal } \frac{n_{i\cdot}}{n},\ \frac{n_{\cdot j}}{n}, \qquad \text{conditional } \frac{n_{ij}}{n_{i\cdot}} \ (F \text{ given } X = x_i), \quad \frac{n_{ij}}{n_{\cdot j}} \ (X \text{ given } F = f_j). \]

The two variables are statistically independent in the sample when all the conditional distributions of \(F\) (the row profiles) coincide, and therefore coincide with the marginal of \(F\). That happens exactly when every cell equals the expected frequency

\[ E_{ij} = \frac{n_{i\cdot}\, n_{\cdot j}}{n}, \]

the count the cell would have if the joint frequency were the product of the marginal frequencies. With real data this equality essentially never holds exactly, even when the underlying variables are independent, because of sampling noise. The question becomes: are the differences \(n_{ij} - E_{ij}\) larger than noise can explain?

The chi-square statistic

Pearson’s statistic adds up the squared differences, each scaled by the expected count:

\[ \chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{c} \frac{(n_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}} = n\Bigl(\sum_{i,j}\frac{n_{ij}^2}{n_{i\cdot}\,n_{\cdot j}} - 1\Bigr). \]

It is 0 only under perfect sample independence and reaches its maximum \(n\,(\min(r,c)-1)\) under perfect dependence (each row profile concentrated on its own column). Because it grows with \(n\), its size is measured on the scale of Cramér’s \(V = \sqrt{\chi^2 / \bigl(n(\min(r,c)-1)\bigr)} \in [0,1]\). The terms \(r_{ij} = (n_{ij}-E_{ij})/\sqrt{E_{ij}}\), the Pearson residuals, show which cells drive the statistic: \(\chi^2 = \sum r_{ij}^2\).

The test

Assume the \(n\) pairs are an i.i.d. sample and take as null hypothesis \(H_0\): \(X\) and \(F\) are independent. Under \(H_0\), as \(n\) grows the statistic follows a chi-square distribution with

\[ \nu = (r-1)(c-1) \]

degrees of freedom: the table has \(rc - 1\) free cells (the total is fixed), and estimating the marginals uses \(r-1\) and \(c-1\) of them, leaving \(rc - 1 - (r-1) - (c-1) = (r-1)(c-1)\). The p-value is the probability, under \(H_0\), of a statistic at least as large as the observed one, \(p = P(\chi^2_\nu \ge \chi^2_{\text{obs}})\). If \(p < \alpha\) (usually 0.05), equivalently if \(\chi^2_{\text{obs}}\) exceeds the critical value \(\chi^2_{\nu,1-\alpha}\), independence is rejected. The approximation is reliable when the expected counts are not too small; the usual rule (Cochran) asks for every \(E_{ij} \ge 1\) and at most 20% of them below 5. When it fails, the p-value can be computed by a permutation test, as this page also does.

Code

The construction needs no library: the table is built by counting the pairs, and only the p-value uses the chi-square survival function from SciPy. The page runs the same algorithm in JavaScript (with its own implementation of the incomplete gamma function for the p-value) on whichever dataset you select below.

from collections import Counter
from scipy.stats import chi2                    # only for the p-value

def contingency(records):                       # records: list of (x, f) pairs
    X = list(dict.fromkeys(x for x, _ in records))    # levels in order of appearance
    F = list(dict.fromkeys(f for _, f in records))
    c = Counter(records)
    n = [[c[(x, f)] for f in F] for x in X]           # n[i][j] = count of (X[i], F[j])
    return X, F, n

def frequencies(n):
    N = sum(map(sum, n))
    row = [sum(r) for r in n]                           # marginal totals of X
    col = [sum(c) for c in zip(*n)]                     # marginal totals of F
    joint  = [[v / N for v in r] for r in n]
    f_of_x = [[v / row[i] for v in r] for i, r in enumerate(n)]          # F | X
    x_of_f = [[n[i][j] / col[j] for j in range(len(col))] for i in range(len(row))]  # X | F
    return row, col, N, joint, f_of_x, x_of_f

def chi_square(n):
    row, col, N = frequencies(n)[:3]
    E = [[row[i] * col[j] / N for j in range(len(col))] for i in range(len(row))]
    stat = sum((n[i][j] - E[i][j]) ** 2 / E[i][j] for i in range(len(row)) for j in range(len(col)))
    df = (len(row) - 1) * (len(col) - 1)
    return stat, df, chi2.sf(stat, df), E

Association is not causation

A significant chi-square says that the two variables are associated: the distribution of one changes with the value of the other. It does not say why. The statistic is symmetric in \(X\) and \(F\), so it cannot even tell which variable could act on the other, and the association can come from a third variable that affects both. The admissions data of the University of California, Berkeley (fall 1973), which Bickel, Hammel and O’Connell analysed in 1975 after the university was worried about sex bias, is the standard example.

The variable behind the paradox is the department. Women applied mostly to departments with low admission rates (C to F), men mostly to departments with high ones (A and B). Department is related to both gender (through the choice of where to apply) and admission, so it produces an association between them in the pooled table even though, inside each department, gender has little or no effect. This reversal of a pooled association when the data are split by a third variable is Simpson’s paradox.

Other ways an association can arise without causation:

  • Reverse causation. The dependence may run from F to X rather than from X to F; the table looks the same either way.
  • Selection. If the records were collected in a way that depends on both variables (only reported incidents, only survivors who answered), the sample can show associations that the population does not have.
  • Chance and multiple testing. At α = 0.05, one test in twenty on independent variables rejects anyway. The simulated dataset with λ = 0 shows this: try several seeds.

Two further cautions about reading the test itself. A small p-value is not a large effect: with thousands of records a weak association becomes highly significant, which is why Cramér’s V is reported next to it. And a large p-value is not proof of independence, only lack of evidence against it, which may simply reflect a small sample. To claim causation one needs a design that breaks the link with other variables, such as a randomised experiment, or an explicit causal model whose assumptions are stated and defended; stratifying by known confounders, as in the Berkeley table, is the first step in that direction.

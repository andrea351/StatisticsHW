1Four ways a sequence of random variables can converge

Let $X_1, X_2,\dots$ and $X$ be random variables defined on the same probability space $(\Omega,\mathcal F,P)$. A random variable is a function $\omega\mapsto X(\omega)$, so “$X_n\to X$” can mean different things. It can be a statement about the functions themselves: pointwise in $\omega$, with high probability, or on average. Or it can be a statement only about their laws. These four readings are the four standard modes of convergence.

Almost sure convergence a.s.

$$P\big(\{\omega:\ \lim_{n\to\infty}X_n(\omega)=X(\omega)\}\big)=1 .$$

Equivalently, for every $\varepsilon\gt 0$, $\;P\big(\sup_{k\ge n}|X_k-X|\gt\varepsilon\big)\to 0$.

Path by path: for almost every realisation, the ordinary numerical sequence $X_n(\omega)$ converges. It constrains the whole tail of the sequence. Typical tool: Borel–Cantelli lemmas.

Convergence in probability P

$$\forall\varepsilon\gt 0:\quad P\big(|X_n-X|\gt\varepsilon\big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0 .$$

One $n$ at a time: for a large but fixed $n$, a deviation larger than $\varepsilon$ is unlikely. It does not say that a given path eventually stays close to $X$; the rare bad indices may keep moving to different $\omega$'s. Typical tool: Markov/Chebyshev inequalities.

Mean-square convergence L²

$$E\big[(X_n-X)^2\big]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0 \qquad(\text{more generally } L^r:\ E|X_n-X|^r\to 0).$$

The average squared error vanishes, so rare but huge errors also count. For an estimator, $E(\hat\theta_n-\theta)^2=\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta_n)+\operatorname{bias}^2(\hat\theta_n)$ is the MSE: L² consistency means that bias and variance both vanish. Typical tool: computing variances.

Convergence in distribution d

$$F_{X_n}(x)\to F_X(x)\ \text{ at every continuity point } x \text{ of } F_X .$$

Equivalent forms: $E\,g(X_n)\to E\,g(X)$ for every bounded continuous $g$ (portmanteau), or $\varphi_{X_n}(t)\to\varphi_X(t)$ for all $t$ (Lévy's continuity theorem). Only the laws matter: the $X_n$ need not even live on the same space as $X$, and nothing is said about $X_n$ being close to $X$. Continuity points are excluded because, e.g., $X_n\equiv 1/n\to 0$ but $F_{X_n}(0)=0\neq 1=F_0(0)$.

2How the modes relate

Solid arrows always hold. Dashed arrows are partial converses that need an extra condition. Every arrow that is missing has a counterexample (§3).

  • a.s. ⇒ P, because $\{|X_n-X|\gt\varepsilon\}\subseteq\{\sup_{k\ge n}|X_k-X|\gt\varepsilon\}$.
  • L² ⇒ P, by Markov/Chebyshev: $P(|X_n-X|\gt\varepsilon)\le E(X_n-X)^2/\varepsilon^2$. Also L² ⇒ L¹, since $E|Y|\le\sqrt{E Y^2}$.
  • P ⇒ d: for any $\varepsilon\gt0$, $F_X(x-\varepsilon)-P(|X_n-X|\gt\varepsilon)\le F_{X_n}(x)\le F_X(x+\varepsilon)+P(|X_n-X|\gt\varepsilon)$; let $n\to\infty$, then $\varepsilon\to0$ at a continuity point.
  • Partial converses. P ⇒ a.s. along a subsequence (Riesz). P + uniform integrability of $|X_n|^2$ ⇒ L² (Vitali), in particular if $|X_n|\le Y$ with $EY^2\lt\infty$ (dominated convergence). d ⇒ P when the limit is a constant $c$, which is how the weak LLN can be proved with characteristic functions. Fast convergence in probability, $\sum_n P(|X_n-X|\gt\varepsilon)\lt\infty$ for every $\varepsilon$, gives a.s. (first Borel–Cantelli lemma). If $X_n\xrightarrow{d}X$, Skorokhod's theorem gives copies with the same laws on another space that converge a.s.

3Counterexamples: the arrows that are missing

(a) In L² and in probability, but not a.s. Take independent $X_n\sim\text{Bernoulli}(1/n)$. Then $E X_n^2=P(X_n=1)=1/n\to0$. But $\sum_n 1/n=\infty$, so by the second Borel–Cantelli lemma (which needs independence) $X_n=1$ infinitely often with probability 1. Every path keeps producing spikes forever, about $\ln 10\approx 2.3$ per decade of $n$, so it never settles at 0. With $\text{Bernoulli}(1/n^2)$ the series $\sum 1/n^2$ converges and the spikes stop: here convergence is a.s.

Fig. 1Spikes of independent Bernoulli(1/n) vs Bernoulli(1/n²) along 12 paths

(b) Almost surely, but not in L² (not even in L¹). Let $U\sim\text{Unif}(0,1)$ and $Y_n=n\,\mathbf 1\{U\le 1/n\}$. For each $\omega$ with $U(\omega)\gt0$, $Y_n(\omega)=0$ as soon as $n\gt1/U(\omega)$, so $Y_n\to0$ a.s. (hence also in probability). Yet $E Y_n=1$ and $E Y_n^2=n\to\infty$: the event where $Y_n\neq0$ shrinks, but its value grows fast enough to keep the mean at 1. This is the typical failure of uniform integrability.

(c) In distribution, but not in probability. Let $X\sim N(0,1)$ and $X_n=-X$ for all $n$. Each $X_n$ has exactly the law of $X$, so $X_n\xrightarrow{d}X$ trivially, but $|X_n-X|=2|X|$ does not shrink. Convergence in distribution says nothing about the random variables themselves being close. The CLT has exactly this feature (§5).

4The Law of Large Numbers: in probability vs almost surely

Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be i.i.d. with $E|X_1|\lt\infty$, $\mu=EX_1$, $S_n=X_1+\dots+X_n$ and $\bar X_n=S_n/n$.

Weak LLN (convergence in probability)

$$\bar X_n\xrightarrow{P}\mu .$$

If $\sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}X_1\lt\infty$ the proof is one line, and it goes through L²: $E(\bar X_n-\mu)^2=\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=\sigma^2/n\to0$, so by Chebyshev $P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\gt\varepsilon)\le \sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2)$. Khinchin's version needs only a finite mean: the characteristic function of $\bar X_n$ tends to $e^{it\mu}$, so $\bar X_n\xrightarrow{d}\mu$, and convergence in distribution to a constant is convergence in probability.

Strong LLN (almost sure convergence)

$$\bar X_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}\mu\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad E|X_1|\lt\infty .$$

Kolmogorov; Etemadi showed that pairwise independence is enough. Almost every infinite sequence of observations has a running average that eventually stays within $\varepsilon$ of $\mu$: $P(\sup_{k\ge n}|\bar X_k-\mu|\gt\varepsilon)\to0$. The WLLN speaks about one large sample size; the SLLN speaks about the whole trajectory of an experimenter who keeps collecting data.

Fig. 2Running means $\bar X_n$, with $\mu\pm2\sigma/\sqrt n$ band and $\mu\pm\varepsilon$ lines

The table estimates two probabilities from 1000 independent paths of length $N=10^4$. The first column is the WLLN quantity at a fixed $n$. The second is the SLLN quantity: the probability that the path leaves the $\varepsilon$-band at some time after $n$, truncated at $N$. The second is always at least as large as the first, and both go to zero. Chebyshev's bound is valid but loose. For the Cauchy distribution $\bar X_n$ is again Cauchy(0,1) for every $n$ (its characteristic function is $e^{-|t|}$, and $(e^{-|t|/n})^n=e^{-|t|}$): averaging does not help at all, and no LLN holds. For Pareto(1.5) the SLLN holds, but convergence is visibly slower than the $\sigma/\sqrt n$ rate, which needs a finite variance.

Statistical role. The LLN is what makes consistency possible. An estimator is weakly consistent if $\hat\theta_n\xrightarrow{P}\theta$ and strongly consistent if $\hat\theta_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}\theta$. Relative frequencies converge to probabilities, which is the frequentist interpretation of probability. Monte Carlo averages converge to integrals. The sample variance converges, $S_n^2\to\sigma^2$ a.s., by the SLLN applied to $X_i$ and $X_i^2$ plus continuity. The empirical distribution function converges uniformly, $\sup_x|\hat F_n(x)-F(x)|\to0$ a.s. (Glivenko–Cantelli, sometimes called the fundamental theorem of statistics).

5The Central Limit Theorem: convergence in distribution only

Lindeberg–Lévy CLT

If $X_i$ are i.i.d. with $0\lt\sigma^2\lt\infty$, then

$$Z_n=\frac{S_n-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}=\frac{\sqrt n\,(\bar X_n-\mu)}{\sigma}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1),\qquad\text{i.e. } P(Z_n\le z)\to\Phi(z)\ \ \forall z .$$

Generalisations: Lindeberg–Feller (independent, not identically distributed), the multivariate CLT, martingale CLTs and CLTs for weakly dependent sequences (§8). Speed: Berry–Esseen, $\sup_z|P(Z_n\le z)-\Phi(z)|\le C\,E|X_1-\mu|^3/(\sigma^3\sqrt n)$ with $C\le0.4748$; again a $\sqrt n$.

The LLN says where $\bar X_n$ goes: to a degenerate limit $\mu$. The CLT says how it fluctuates around $\mu$, at scale $\sigma/\sqrt n$ and with a Gaussian shape whatever the law of $X_1$. Informally, $\bar X_n\approx\mu+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}Z$ with $Z\sim N(0,1)$.

Why it cannot be upgraded to convergence in probability. Split $S_{2n}$ into the first and second half: $Z_{2n}=(Z_n+Z'_n)/\sqrt2$, where $Z'_n$ is built from $X_{n+1},\dots,X_{2n}$ and is independent of $Z_n$. Hence $\operatorname{Corr}(Z_n,Z_{2n})=1/\sqrt2$ for every $n$, and $Z_{2n}-Z_n\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\,2-\sqrt2)$, not $0$. So $(Z_n)$ is not Cauchy in probability, and there is no random variable it converges to. Path-wise it is even worse. The law of the iterated logarithm, $\limsup_n \frac{S_n-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{2n\log\log n}}=1$ a.s., implies $\limsup Z_n=+\infty$ and $\liminf Z_n=-\infty$ a.s.: every path of $Z_n$ oscillates forever, and only the histogram across paths settles. This is counterexample (c) of §3 appearing in a central theorem.

7Why √n: variances add, standard deviations do not

For any random variables with finite variances,

$$\operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname{Var}(X_i)+2\sum_{i\lt j}\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j).$$

If the $X_i$ are uncorrelated (in particular, independent) with common variance $\sigma^2$, all cross terms vanish and

$$\operatorname{Var}(S_n)=n\sigma^2,\qquad \operatorname{sd}(S_n)=\sigma\sqrt n .$$

Geometric view. Centred variables with finite variance are vectors in the Hilbert space $L^2$, with inner product $\langle X,Y\rangle=\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)$ and length $\|X\|=\operatorname{sd}(X)$. Uncorrelated means orthogonal, and by Pythagoras the sum of $n$ orthogonal vectors of length $\sigma$ has length $\sigma\sqrt n$, like the diagonal of an $n$-dimensional cube. If all the vectors pointed the same way (perfect correlation) the length would be $n\sigma$. Independent errors partly cancel each other, and $\sqrt n$ measures exactly how much.

Three normalisations. The signal $n\mu$ grows like $n$, the noise like $\sqrt n$. Dividing the centred sum by $n^\alpha$ gives standard deviation $\sigma n^{1/2-\alpha}$:

$$\operatorname{sd}(S_n-n\mu)=\sigma\sqrt n\to\infty,\qquad \operatorname{sd}(\bar X_n-\mu)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\to0\ \ (\text{LLN}),\qquad \operatorname{sd}\big(\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)\big)=\sigma\ \ (\text{CLT}).$$

With $\alpha\lt\tfrac12$ the spread explodes; with $\alpha\gt\tfrac12$ it collapses to 0 (that is the LLN, a degenerate limit). Only $\alpha=\tfrac12$ gives a non-trivial limit, and the CLT adds that its shape is Gaussian. So $\sqrt n$ is the natural scale of the fluctuations of a sum of $n$ weakly dependent terms with finite variance.

Consequences for statistical inference

  • Standard error. $\operatorname{SE}(\bar X_n)=\sigma/\sqrt n$, estimated by $S_n/\sqrt n$. A large-sample confidence interval $\bar X_n\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,S_n/\sqrt n$ (valid by CLT + Slutsky) has width $\propto1/\sqrt n$: halving the error needs four times the data. The sample size for a margin $E$ is $n\ge(z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma/E)^2$.
  • Tests. $T=\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu_0)/S_n$. Under an alternative $\mu_1$, $T\approx N\big(\sqrt n(\mu_1-\mu_0)/\sigma,\,1\big)$: the non-centrality grows like $\sqrt n$, so power $\to1$ and the smallest detectable effect shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$. This is also why, with very large samples, practically negligible effects become “statistically significant”.
  • Efficiency. In regular parametric models the best possible variance is the Cramér–Rao bound $1/(nI(\theta))$, so good estimators are “$\sqrt n$-consistent”. Faster or slower rates appear only when the assumptions break: $\max_i X_i$ for $\text{Unif}(0,\theta)$ converges at rate $n$ (non-regular model), and kernel density estimators at rate $n^{2/5}$ (nonparametric).
  • Monte Carlo. A simulation average with $m$ replications has error $\sigma/\sqrt m$ in any dimension. That is why every simulated table in these homeworks carries an uncertainty of order $1/\sqrt m$.
  • Bayesian inference. The posterior standard deviation also shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$ (Bernstein–von Mises; Fig. 9 in Ex. 2).

8The role of independence (and what happens without it)

What $\sqrt n$ really needs is that $\operatorname{Var}(S_n)$ grows linearly in $n$. Independence is the simplest sufficient condition, but it is neither necessary for the variance formula (uncorrelatedness is enough, and also gives the WLLN through Chebyshev) nor sufficient alone (a finite variance is needed too). The CLT needs more than uncorrelatedness, for example independence, a martingale structure or mixing.

  • Weak dependence. For a stationary sequence with autocorrelations $\rho_k$ that are summable, $\operatorname{Var}(S_n)/n\to\sigma^2\big(1+2\sum_{k\ge1}\rho_k\big)=\sigma^2_{\text{LR}}$ (long-run variance). The rate is still $\sqrt n$, but with a different constant. For an AR(1) with coefficient $\rho$ the factor is $(1+\rho)/(1-\rho)$, and the effective sample size is $n_{\text{eff}}\approx n\,(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)$. Using $\sigma/\sqrt n$ while ignoring positive correlation gives standard errors that are too small and intervals that are too narrow. This matters for time series, clustered data and MCMC output (Fig. 5).
  • Strong dependence. If $X_i=X_1$ for all $i$, then $\bar X_n=X_1$: no LLN at all, because $\operatorname{Var}(S_n)=n^2\sigma^2$. With long-range dependence (fractional noise, Hurst exponent $H\in(\tfrac12,1)$), $\operatorname{Var}(S_n)\propto n^{2H}$ and $\bar X_n$ converges only at rate $n^{1-H}$, slower than $\sqrt n$.
  • Negative dependence helps. When sampling without replacement from a population of size $N$, $\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\cdot\frac{N-n}{N-1}$, the finite-population correction.
  • Infinite variance. Independence alone is not enough. If the tails are heavy, $P(|X|\gt x)\sim c\,x^{-\alpha}$ with $1\lt\alpha\lt2$, the generalised CLT gives $(S_n-n\mu)/n^{1/\alpha}\xrightarrow{d}$ an $\alpha$-stable law, so $\bar X_n-\mu$ shrinks only like $n^{1/\alpha-1}$ (for Pareto 1.5, like $n^{-1/3}$; see Figs. 2–4). For the Cauchy distribution ($\alpha=1$) it does not shrink at all.

9Summary

  • a.s. concerns whole paths, P concerns one $n$ at a time, L² concerns average squared size, and d concerns only laws. a.s. ⇒ P ⇐ L², and P ⇒ d. The other directions need extra conditions (subsequence, uniform integrability, constant limit).
  • LLN: $\bar X_n\to\mu$ in L² and in P (weak, via Chebyshev) and a.s. (strong). It underlies consistency, Glivenko–Cantelli and Monte Carlo.
  • CLT: $\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)/\sigma\to N(0,1)$ only in distribution. The normalised sums never settle path-wise (LIL), but their law does. With Slutsky and the delta method it underlies intervals, tests and the asymptotics of the MLE.
  • √n comes from the additivity of variance for uncorrelated (in particular independent) terms: $\operatorname{sd}(S_n)=\sigma\sqrt n$. It is the only scaling with a non-degenerate limit. Dependence changes the constant (long-run variance, $n_{\text{eff}}$) or the rate, and heavy tails change the rate to $n^{1-1/\alpha}$.

1Conditional probability

$$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B)\gt0 .$$

Learning that $B$ occurred restricts the sample space to $B$ and renormalises it so that the total mass is again 1. $P(\,\cdot\mid B)$ is itself a probability measure, so all the rules of probability apply to it.

  • Multiplication (chain) rule: $P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)P(B)$, and $P(A_1\cap\dots\cap A_n)=\prod_k P(A_k\mid A_1\cap\dots\cap A_{k-1})$.
  • Law of total probability: for a partition $\{B_j\}$ of $\Omega$, $P(A)=\sum_j P(A\mid B_j)P(B_j)$.
  • Independence: $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$: learning $B$ changes nothing. Conditional independence, $P(A\cap C\mid B)=P(A\mid B)P(C\mid B)$, is the key notion in statistics: observations are usually modelled as i.i.d. given the parameter (de Finetti's theorem links this to exchangeability).
  • Asymmetry: in general $P(A\mid B)\neq P(B\mid A)$. Confusing the two is the prosecutor's fallacy, or base-rate neglect (§5). Bayes' theorem is exactly the rule that converts one into the other.

In probability theory, the idea extends to random variables. Conditional densities are $f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)=f_{X,Y}(x,y)/f_X(x)$. The conditional expectation $E[Y\mid X]$ is the best predictor of $Y$ from $X$ in mean square (an orthogonal projection in $L^2$, the same geometry as in Ex. 1, §7). It satisfies the tower property $E\,E[Y\mid X]=EY$ and the law of total variance $\operatorname{Var}Y=E\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X)+\operatorname{Var}E(Y\mid X)$. Markov chains and martingales are defined through conditioning.

In statistics, a model is a family of conditional distributions $f(x\mid\theta)$ of the data given the parameter. The two schools condition in opposite directions:

Frequentist

$\theta$ is fixed and unknown. Probabilities refer to repeated samples given $\theta$: a p-value is $P(T\ge t_{\text{obs}}\mid H_0)$, and coverage is $P(\theta\in C(X)\mid\theta)$. Regression models $E[Y\mid X=x]$, and sufficiency is defined by $X\mid T$ not depending on $\theta$.

Bayesian

Uncertainty about $\theta$ is itself described by a probability distribution, and inference conditions on the data actually observed: the object of interest is $\pi(\theta\mid x)$. Moving from $f(x\mid\theta)$ to $\pi(\theta\mid x)$ is what Bayes' theorem does.

2Bayes' theorem

Writing $P(H_i\cap E)$ in two ways, $P(E\mid H_i)P(H_i)=P(H_i\mid E)P(E)$, and expanding $P(E)$ by total probability over a partition of hypotheses $\{H_j\}$:

$$P(H_i\mid E)=\frac{P(E\mid H_i)\,P(H_i)}{\sum_j P(E\mid H_j)\,P(H_j)} .$$

For a continuous parameter $\theta$ with prior density $\pi(\theta)$ and data $x$:

$$\underbrace{\pi(\theta\mid x)}_{\text{posterior}}=\frac{\overbrace{f(x\mid\theta)}^{\text{likelihood}}\;\overbrace{\pi(\theta)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{m(x)}_{\text{evidence}}},\qquad m(x)=\int f(x\mid\theta')\,\pi(\theta')\,d\theta',\qquad\text{so}\quad \pi(\theta\mid x)\propto f(x\mid\theta)\,\pi(\theta).$$

Odds form. For two hypotheses,

$$\frac{P(H_1\mid E)}{P(H_0\mid E)}=\underbrace{\frac{P(E\mid H_1)}{P(E\mid H_0)}}_{\text{likelihood ratio (Bayes factor)}}\times\frac{P(H_1)}{P(H_0)} .$$

In logarithms, log posterior odds = log prior odds + log likelihood ratio. Independent pieces of evidence simply add their log likelihood ratios. With many observations, this sum obeys the LLN of Ex. 1 (§4 below).

3The three components: prior, likelihood, posterior

Prior $\pi(\theta)$

What is known or believed about $\theta$ before the current data. Sources: previous studies, expert knowledge, physical constraints, or a deliberately vague choice.

  • Informative: concentrated, encodes real knowledge.
  • Weakly informative: rules out absurd values only.
  • Non-informative / reference: uniform (but not invariant under reparametrisation); Jeffreys $\pi(\theta)\propto\sqrt{I(\theta)}$ (invariant).
  • Conjugate: the posterior stays in the same family (Beta–Binomial, Normal–Normal, Gamma–Poisson).
  • Improper ($\int\pi=\infty$): allowed only if the posterior is proper.

Likelihood $L(\theta;x)=f(x\mid\theta)$

The sampling model read as a function of $\theta$, with the data held fixed. It is not a density in $\theta$ (it need not integrate to 1). For i.i.d. data

$$\ell(\theta)=\log L(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\log f(x_i\mid\theta),$$

a sum of $n$ terms, so it grows like $n$ while the log prior stays fixed. It is the only channel through which data enter (likelihood principle). It depends on the data only through sufficient statistics, and it is shared with frequentist inference (MLE, likelihood-ratio tests).

Posterior $\pi(\theta\mid x)$

The complete updated state of uncertainty about $\theta$. Summaries:

  • posterior mean: Bayes estimator under squared loss; median: under absolute loss;
  • MAP (mode) = penalised MLE, $\arg\max\{\ell(\theta)+\log\pi(\theta)\}$;
  • credible interval: $P(a\le\theta\le b\mid x)=0.95$, a direct probability statement about $\theta$;
  • posterior predictive: $p(\tilde x\mid x)=\int f(\tilde x\mid\theta)\pi(\theta\mid x)d\theta$.

The evidence $m(x)$ does not matter for inference on $\theta$ within one model, but it is central for comparing models: Bayes factor $B_{10}=m_1(x)/m_0(x)$.

How they relate. The prior and the likelihood are the two inputs, one from before the data and one from the data, and the posterior is their normalised product. Where the likelihood is flat (data uninformative), the posterior follows the prior. Where it is sharp (many data), the posterior follows the likelihood. In between, the posterior is a compromise weighted by the relative precision of the two sources (§6 and §7 make the weights explicit).

4Updating, and why it is central

Sequential updating. If $x_1,x_2$ are conditionally independent given $\theta$,

$$\pi(\theta\mid x_1,x_2)\propto f(x_2\mid\theta)\,f(x_1\mid\theta)\,\pi(\theta)\propto f(x_2\mid\theta)\;\underbrace{\pi(\theta\mid x_1)}_{\text{yesterday's posterior}} .$$

Yesterday's posterior is today's prior. The result is the same whether the data arrive one at a time or all together, and in any order. Learning is therefore coherent: an agent who updates this way cannot be made to accept a set of bets that loses for sure (Dutch-book argument).

Why it is central to Bayesian inference.

  1. One rule for every problem. Estimation, interval estimation, testing, prediction and decisions all come from the posterior, combined with a loss function where a decision is needed.
  2. Direct answers. It gives $P(\theta\in A\mid\text{data})$ and $P(H\mid\text{data})$, which is what p-values and confidence intervals are often (wrongly) read as.
  3. Information is accumulated. Prior studies, constraints and partial pooling in hierarchical models (borrowing strength between groups) enter naturally. This is especially useful with small samples.
  4. Data eventually dominate. By the LLN of Ex. 1, $\frac1n\ell_n(\theta)\to E_{\theta_0}\log f(X\mid\theta)$ a.s., and this limit is maximised at the true $\theta_0$ because $E_{\theta_0}\log\frac{f(X\mid\theta_0)}{f(X\mid\theta)}=\mathrm{KL}(\theta_0\,\|\,\theta)\ge0$. Writing $\pi(\theta\mid x_{1:n})\propto\exp\{n\cdot\tfrac1n\ell_n(\theta)+\log\pi(\theta)\}$, the data term grows like $n$ while the prior term stays $O(1)$. The posterior therefore concentrates at $\theta_0$ (Doob, Schwartz) whenever $\pi(\theta_0)\gt0$; a value with zero prior mass can never be recovered (Cromwell's rule).
  5. Same √n as in Ex. 1. Bernstein–von Mises: in regular models $\pi(\theta\mid x_{1:n})\approx N\big(\hat\theta_n,\,[nI(\theta_0)]^{-1}\big)$. The posterior sd shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$, and Bayesian credible intervals and frequentist confidence intervals agree asymptotically.

5Example 1: base rates and diagnostic tests (the prior matters a lot)

A test for a condition $D$ has sensitivity $P(+\mid D)$ and specificity $P(-\mid\bar D)$. The prior is the prevalence $P(D)$, the likelihood of a positive result is $P(+\mid D)$ vs $P(+\mid\bar D)$, and the posterior is the positive predictive value

$$\text{PPV}=P(D\mid +)=\frac{P(+\mid D)P(D)}{P(+\mid D)P(D)+P(+\mid\bar D)\,(1-P(D))} .$$

With the default values (prevalence 1%, sensitivity 99%, specificity 95%) a positive result raises the probability of disease from 1% only to about 17%. Among 1000 people there are about 10 true positives but about 50 false positives. The likelihood ratio $LR^+=0.99/0.05=19.8$ is strong evidence, but it multiplies prior odds of $1{:}99$. A second independent positive test uses 17% as its prior: the odds become $0.2\times19.8\approx3.96$, i.e. about 80%. This is sequential updating in its simplest form.

Application in cybersecurity: the base-rate fallacy of intrusion detection (Axelsson, 2000). Suppose 1 event in 10,000 is malicious and an IDS detects 99% of attacks with a 1% false-alarm rate. Then

$$P(\text{attack}\mid\text{alarm})=\frac{0.99\cdot10^{-4}}{0.99\cdot10^{-4}+0.01\cdot(1-10^{-4})}\approx0.98\% ,$$

so about 99 alarms out of 100 are false. Because attacks are rare, it is the false-alarm rate, not the detection rate, that limits the usefulness of an IDS: it has to be of the same order as the base rate. The same logic applies to spam filters, fraud detection and mass screening.

Limitations shown by this example. The right prior depends on the population: the same positive test means much more for a patient with symptoms (high pre-test probability) than in mass screening. The repeated-test calculation assumes that the tests are conditionally independent given the true state; if a false positive has a systematic cause, the second test adds much less evidence than the formula suggests.

6Example 2: Beta–Binomial, estimating a success probability

Continuing the theme of the earlier homeworks: $\theta$ is the probability that a single penetration attempt succeeds, and we observe $k$ successes in $n$ independent attempts.

$$\underbrace{\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k}}_{\text{likelihood}}\ \times\ \underbrace{\frac{\theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}}{B(a,b)}}_{\text{prior Beta}(a,b)}\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \theta\mid\text{data}\sim\text{Beta}(a+k,\;b+n-k).$$

The prior acts as $a$ pseudo-successes and $b$ pseudo-failures: its weight in the analysis is like $a+b$ extra observations. The posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and the MLE:

$$E[\theta\mid\text{data}]=\frac{a+k}{a+b+n}=w\cdot\frac{a}{a+b}+(1-w)\cdot\frac{k}{n},\qquad w=\frac{a+b}{a+b+n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,$$

and the posterior variance is $\frac{m(1-m)}{a+b+n+1}\approx\frac{\theta(1-\theta)}{n}$, with $m$ the posterior mean. That is the same $1/\sqrt n$ as in Ex. 1.

Sequential = batch. The table shows the first ten attempts processed one at a time with the selected prior: each step adds 1 to $a$ (success) or to $b$ (failure). After all $n$ attempts the result is $\text{Beta}(a+k,\,b+n-k)$, exactly the one-shot posterior.

What the figures show about the influence of the prior.

  • For small n the prior matters: with $n=20$ the four posterior means can differ by more than one posterior sd. The well-centred expert prior gives the most accurate estimate (narrower interval, close to the truth). The overconfident wrong prior pulls the estimate towards 0.7 and its credible interval may miss the true value: this is a prior–data conflict.
  • For large n all posteriors merge with each other and with the MLE, and their sd decreases like $\sqrt{\theta(1-\theta)/n}$ (dashed line in Fig. 9). The prior is “washed out” at rate $(a+b)/n$.
  • Edge case: $k=0$ successes in $n=10$. The MLE is $0$ and the Wald interval $\hat\theta\pm1.96\sqrt{\hat\theta(1-\hat\theta)/n}$ is the absurd $[0,0]$. Under the flat prior the posterior is $\text{Beta}(1,11)$, with mean $1/12\approx0.083$ and 95% upper bound $1-0.05^{1/11}\approx0.24$. This is a sensible statement of “rare, but not impossible”, and a case where the prior regularises a degenerate estimate.

7Example 3: Normal–Normal, a precision-weighted compromise

Measurements $x_1,\dots,x_n\mid\mu\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ with $\sigma$ known, and prior $\mu\sim N(\mu_0,\tau_0^2)$. Completing the square in $\exp\{-\sum(x_i-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2-(\mu-\mu_0)^2/2\tau_0^2\}$ gives $\mu\mid x\sim N(\mu_n,\tau_n^2)$ with

$$\frac1{\tau_n^2}=\underbrace{\frac1{\tau_0^2}}_{\text{prior precision}}+\underbrace{\frac{n}{\sigma^2}}_{\text{data precision}},\qquad \mu_n=\tau_n^2\Big(\frac{\mu_0}{\tau_0^2}+\frac{n\bar x}{\sigma^2}\Big).$$

Precisions add (just as variances add in Ex. 1), and the posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the sample mean. Numerical example: $\sigma=2$, prior $N(10,1)$, and the observed mean is $\bar x=12$.

Applications include sensor fusion and the Kalman filter, which is this formula applied recursively at each time step, shrinkage estimators, and empirical Bayes. Link with machine learning: the MAP estimate under a Gaussian prior on regression coefficients is ridge regression, and under a Laplace prior it is the lasso. Regularisation is a prior.

8Advantages, limitations and influence on conclusions

Advantages

  • Coherent, unified framework: everything follows from one rule.
  • Intuitive outputs: probabilities of hypotheses and credible intervals.
  • Uses prior information explicitly and transparently, which helps with small samples and rare events (§6, $k=0$).
  • Natural sequential learning, as in online monitoring, adaptive trials and A/B tests.
  • Hierarchical models borrow strength across groups.
  • Propagates uncertainty to predictions (posterior predictive).
  • Regularisation: priors stabilise ill-posed or high-dimensional problems.

Limitations

  • Subjectivity: with little data, different priors lead to different conclusions (Figs. 7–8). Remedy: state the prior and run a sensitivity analysis.
  • “Non-informative” is not unique: flat on $\theta$ is not flat on $\log\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}$. Jeffreys priors fix invariance but can be improper.
  • Zero prior mass can never be corrected (Cromwell's rule). Improper priors can give improper posteriors.
  • Bayes factors stay sensitive to the prior even for large $n$ (Jeffreys–Lindley paradox).
  • Computation: $m(x)$ is usually intractable, so MCMC or variational methods are needed. MCMC output is dependent, so its error is governed by $n_{\text{eff}}$ (Ex. 1, Fig. 5).
  • Misspecified likelihood: the posterior concentrates on the KL-closest wrong value, and a prior cannot fix a wrong model.

Influence on statistical conclusions, in short. The posterior is a compromise whose weights are explicit: $w=(a+b)/(a+b+n)$ in the Beta–Binomial model, $\tau_0^{-2}/(\tau_0^{-2}+n\sigma^{-2})$ in the Normal–Normal model. So the prior decides the conclusion when the effective prior sample size is comparable to $n$, and becomes irrelevant when $n$ is much larger. With rare events (§5) the prior (base rate) dominates even when the evidence is strong. The likelihood determines the shape and rate of learning: a sharper likelihood (more data, more informative experiments, larger $I(\theta)$) means faster concentration, at the $\sqrt n$ rate of Ex. 1.

9Summary

  • Conditional probability restricts and renormalises the sample space. Statistics models data given parameters, and Bayesian inference reverses the conditioning with Bayes' theorem.
  • Posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. In odds form, posterior odds = Bayes factor × prior odds; evidence adds on the log scale.
  • Updating is sequential and coherent: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior.
  • The prior matters when data are few or events are rare (base rates). As $n$ grows the likelihood dominates (LLN) and the posterior becomes approximately normal with sd $\propto1/\sqrt n$ (Bernstein–von Mises), which connects this exercise to Ex. 1.

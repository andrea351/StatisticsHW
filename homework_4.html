Ex. 1

Welford’s recurrence formulas

Derive Welford’s recurrence formulas for the sum of squares (SS) and the variance, following the same approach as Knuth’s recurrence formula for the mean. Write the corresponding code. The core logic should require only about 3 lines.

Notation

We receive the observations \(x_1, x_2, \dots\) one at a time and want, after the \(k\)-th one, the running mean, the sum of squared deviations and the variance, without storing the data and without a second pass:

\[ M_k = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} x_i, \qquad SS_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \bigl(x_i - M_k\bigr)^2, \qquad s_k^2 = \frac{SS_k}{k-1}\ \ (k \ge 2). \]

Throughout, \(\delta_k = x_k - M_{k-1}\) is the deviation of the new observation from the old mean. It is the only new quantity each step needs.

Starting point: Knuth’s recurrence for the mean

The idea is to write the step-\(k\) quantity using the step-\((k-1)\) quantity plus a correction that involves only \(x_k\). For the mean, split off the last term of the sum:

\[ k\,M_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} x_i + x_k = (k-1)\,M_{k-1} + x_k = k\,M_{k-1} + \bigl(x_k - M_{k-1}\bigr). \]

Dividing by \(k\):

\[ \boxed{\,M_k = M_{k-1} + \frac{x_k - M_{k-1}}{k} = M_{k-1} + \frac{\delta_k}{k}\,}, \qquad M_0 = 0. \]

Two consequences are used below:

\[ M_k - M_{k-1} = \frac{\delta_k}{k}, \qquad x_k - M_k = \delta_k - \frac{\delta_k}{k} = \frac{k-1}{k}\,\delta_k . \tag{1} \]

Recurrence for the sum of squares

Same approach: split off the last term, and rewrite the first \(k-1\) deviations around the old mean \(M_{k-1}\), whose deviations we already know sum to \(SS_{k-1}\). For \(i \le k-1\),

\[ x_i - M_k = \bigl(x_i - M_{k-1}\bigr) - \bigl(M_k - M_{k-1}\bigr) = \bigl(x_i - M_{k-1}\bigr) - \frac{\delta_k}{k}. \]

Squaring and summing over the first \(k-1\) terms:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\bigl(x_i - M_k\bigr)^2 = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\bigl(x_i - M_{k-1}\bigr)^2}_{SS_{k-1}} \;-\; \frac{2\delta_k}{k}\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\bigl(x_i - M_{k-1}\bigr)}_{=\,0} \;+\; (k-1)\frac{\delta_k^2}{k^2}. \]

The cross term vanishes because deviations from a mean always sum to zero. Adding the last term \((x_k-M_k)^2 = \frac{(k-1)^2}{k^2}\delta_k^2\) from (1):

\[ SS_k = SS_{k-1} + \frac{(k-1)\,\delta_k^2}{k^2}\bigl(1 + (k-1)\bigr) = SS_{k-1} + \frac{k-1}{k}\,\delta_k^2 . \]

Finally, by (1), \(\frac{k-1}{k}\delta_k = x_k - M_k\), so one factor \(\delta_k\) can be replaced by the deviation from the new mean. This is Welford’s formula:

\[ \boxed{\,SS_k = SS_{k-1} + \bigl(x_k - M_{k-1}\bigr)\bigl(x_k - M_k\bigr) = SS_{k-1} + \delta_k\,\bigl(x_k - M_k\bigr)\,}, \qquad SS_0 = 0. \]

Check at \(k=1\): \(M_1 = x_1\), so \(SS_1 = 0 + (x_1 - 0)(x_1 - x_1) = 0\), as it should be. Each increment is a product of two numbers with the same sign (by (1) both are multiples of \(\delta_k\)), so \(SS_k\) never decreases and can never become negative because of rounding.

Recurrence for the variance

Dividing \(SS_k\) by the right count gives the variance directly. With the unbiased divisor \(k-1\), from \(SS_k = SS_{k-1} + \frac{k-1}{k}\delta_k^2\) and \(SS_{k-1} = (k-2)\,s_{k-1}^2\):

\[ \boxed{\,s_k^2 = \frac{k-2}{k-1}\,s_{k-1}^2 + \frac{\delta_k^2}{k}\,}, \qquad k \ge 2 \quad (s_2^2 = \delta_2^2/2). \]

With divisor \(k\) (the variance of the \(k\) values seen so far, \(\hat\sigma_k^2 = SS_k/k\)) the recurrence takes exactly the shape of Knuth’s formula, old value plus (new term − old value)/k:

\[ \boxed{\,\hat\sigma_k^2 = \hat\sigma_{k-1}^2 + \frac{\delta_k\,(x_k - M_k) - \hat\sigma_{k-1}^2}{k}\,}. \]

This is no coincidence. Unrolling Welford’s formula gives \(SS_k = \sum_{i\le k}\delta_i\,(x_i - M_i)\), so \(\hat\sigma_k^2\) is simply the running mean of the terms \(\delta_i(x_i-M_i)\), and Knuth’s recurrence applies to it as it does to any mean.

In practice it is simpler to keep \(SS_k\) and divide only when the variance is needed, which is what the code does.

Code

The core logic is three lines, executed once per observation. Their order matters: \(\delta_k\) uses the old mean, the last line uses the new one.

def welford(xs):
    k, mean, ss = 0, 0.0, 0.0
    for x in xs:
        k += 1
        d = x - mean            # δ_k = x_k − M_(k−1)
        mean += d / k           # Knuth:   M_k  = M_(k−1) + δ_k / k
        ss += d * (x - mean)    # Welford: SS_k = SS_(k−1) + δ_k (x_k − M_k)
    var = ss / (k - 1) if k > 1 else float("nan")
    return mean, ss, var

The same three lines in JavaScript, as used by the simulation in Ex. 2 of this page:

function welfordUpdate(st, x) {   // st = { k, mean, ss }
  st.k += 1;
  const d = x - st.mean;
  st.mean += d / st.k;
  st.ss += d * (x - st.mean);
}
// sample variance: st.ss / (st.k - 1)

Step by step on your own numbers

Why not the textbook formula

The one-pass shortcut \(SS_k = \sum x_i^2 - k\,M_k^2\) gives the same number in exact arithmetic, but in floating point it subtracts two huge, nearly equal quantities when the data have a large mean compared with their spread. All the significant digits cancel. Welford’s update only ever works with deviations \(\delta_k\), which stay small. The table runs both on the four values \(C+4,\ C+7,\ C+13,\ C+16\) in double precision; the exact sample variance is 30 for every offset \(C\).

B. P. Welford, “Note on a method for calculating corrected sums of squares and products”, Technometrics 4(3), 1962. D. E. Knuth, The Art of Computer Programming, Vol. 2, 3rd ed., §4.2.2.
Ex. 2

Sampling mean and variance

Each penetration attempt gets a score between 0 and 10, drawn from a fixed distribution with mean μ and variance σ². There are m hackers, each attacking n systems, so each hacker has a trajectory of n scores. For every hacker, compute the sampling mean and sampling variance with Welford’s recurrences; then average them over the m hackers and compare with μ and σ². Repeat for different n and study how n affects the variability of the sampling means and variances.

Model

The scores \(X_{j,1},\dots,X_{j,n}\) of hacker \(j\) are i.i.d. with distribution \(F\) on \([0,10]\), and different hackers are independent. For each hacker, Welford’s recurrences give

\[ \bar X_{j} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_{j,i}, \qquad s_j^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n \bigl(X_{j,i}-\bar X_j\bigr)^2, \]

and across the hackers we compare \(\overline{\bar X} = \frac1m\sum_j \bar X_j\) with \(\mu\) and \(\overline{s^2} = \frac1m\sum_j s_j^2\) with \(\sigma^2\). As a contrast, the page also keeps the variance with divisor \(n\), \(\hat\sigma_j^2 = SS_j/n\).

The default distribution is \(\text{Binomial}(10,\,0.3)\): each attack goes through 10 independent stages (scan, foothold, privilege escalation, …), each passed with probability 0.3, and the score is the number of stages passed. It gives integer scores from 0 to 10 with \(\mu = 3\), \(\sigma^2 = 2.1\). The other options let you check that nothing below depends on that choice.

The first 30 hackers at the largest n. Each line is a running estimate as the hacker moves from one system to the next, updated with the three Welford lines; the horizontal axis is logarithmic. Every line wanders at the start and settles near the true value, inside the band \(\mu \pm 2\sigma/\sqrt{k}\) for the mean.

Averages across hackers

Each row is an independent experiment with m new hackers. The z columns measure the distance from the theoretical value in units of the Monte Carlo standard error, \(\sigma/\sqrt{mn}\) for the mean and \(\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(s^2)/m}\) for the variance: values within about ±2 mean the difference is just simulation noise.

Spread across hackers

The empirical variance, over the m hackers, of the sampling means and of the sampling variances (computed, again, with Welford’s recurrences), next to the theoretical values

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\bar X) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(s^2) = \frac1n\Bigl(\mu_4 - \frac{n-3}{n-1}\,\sigma^4\Bigr), \qquad \mu_4 = \mathbb E\bigl[(X-\mu)^4\bigr]. \]

Conclusions

What the comparisons show

The average of the sampling means matches \(\mu\) at every n, including n = 2, within its Monte Carlo error. This is unbiasedness of the sample mean, \(\mathbb E[\bar X] = \frac1n\sum_i \mathbb E[X_i] = \mu\), which holds for any n and any distribution with a finite mean.

The average of the sampling variances with divisor \(n-1\) also matches \(\sigma^2\) at every n. The reason is

\[ \mathbb E[SS_n] = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb E\bigl[(X_i-\mu)^2\bigr] - n\,\mathbb E\bigl[(\bar X-\mu)^2\bigr] = n\sigma^2 - \sigma^2 = (n-1)\,\sigma^2, \]

so \(\mathbb E[s^2] = \sigma^2\). Deviations are measured from \(\bar X\), which is fitted to the same data and is therefore closer to them than \(\mu\) is; one degree of freedom is spent estimating the mean. With divisor n the average lands on \(\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2\) instead: at n = 2 it is half of \(\sigma^2\), at n = 1000 the bias is negligible. The orange points follow that curve exactly.

The match holds for the average over hackers, not for each hacker. At small n a single hacker’s mean or variance can be far from the truth, as the trajectories and histograms show. Unbiasedness is a property of the estimator over repeated samples.

Why this is useful in inferential statistics

In practice we see one sample, not m of them, and μ and σ² are unknown. The m simulated hackers make the sampling distribution visible: the distribution that \(\bar X\) and \(s^2\) would have over repeated samples. Every inferential statement is a statement about that distribution.

  • Unbiasedness justifies \(\bar X\) and \(s^2\) as point estimates of μ and σ²: on average they neither overestimate nor underestimate.
  • The variance of \(\bar X\) is \(\sigma^2/n\). Replacing σ² with the unbiased \(s^2\) gives the standard error \(s/\sqrt n\), which measures how far one sample mean is likely to be from μ.
  • With the central limit theorem (top row of histograms, close to the normal curve already at moderate n), this leads to confidence intervals \(\bar X \pm z_{\alpha/2}\, s/\sqrt n\) and to t tests. For example, the mean score of one hacker over 100 systems comes with a known margin of error.
  • Welford’s recurrences let a monitoring system update these estimates attempt by attempt, without storing the scores and without the cancellation problem of Ex. 1.

Effect of the number of systems n

Increasing n does not move the centre of either sampling distribution: they are already centred on μ and σ² for every n. What it changes is their spread. Both variances fall like \(1/n\), a slope of −1 in the log–log plot, so the standard deviations fall like \(1/\sqrt n\): four times as many systems halve the typical error of a hacker’s estimate. Both estimators are consistent; as n grows they concentrate on μ and σ², as in the law of large numbers of the previous homework.

The variance estimate is noisier than the mean estimate, and its noise depends on the fourth central moment \(\mu_4\), that is, on the tails of the score distribution. For the skewed distribution the \(s_j^2\) histograms stay skewed to the right up to large n, while those of \(\bar X_j\) are already nearly normal. Increasing m does not change any of this: m only sharpens our view of the sampling distribution (smaller z noise), while the precision of each hacker’s own estimates depends on n alone.

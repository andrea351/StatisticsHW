Penetration scores as random walks

How the penetration score of m independent attackers evolves across n systems, and what the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem predict for its total, its average and its √n-normalized version.

The model

Think of an attack campaign as a sequence of steps, one per system. Attacker j moves through systems 1, 2, …, n and at step i collects a score Xi(j). The scores are independent and identically distributed, across steps and across attackers, so the m attackers are m independent copies of the same random walk k ↦ Sk. Collected together, the increments form an n × m matrix: each column is one attacker's trajectory, each row is one system as seen by all the attackers.

Bernoulli model. Xi = 1 means that the attacker compromised system i (probability p), Xi = 0 that it failed. Then Sn counts the systems compromised by one attacker and is Binomial(n, p), with μ = E[Xi] = p and σ² = Var(Xi) = p(1 − p).

Rademacher model. Xi = +1 is progress (a foothold gained) and Xi = −1 a setback (detection, a foothold lost), each with probability 1/2. Now μ = 0 and σ² = 1, and Sn is the simple symmetric random walk: Sn = 2B − n with B ~ Binomial(n, 1/2), so Sn always has the parity of n. This is the case without drift, where only fluctuations are left.

Because the Xi are independent, means and variances add: E[Sn] = nμ and Var(Sn) = nσ². Dividing by n or by √n only rescales these two numbers, and this already contains the three behaviours the homework asks about.

So √n is the only scaling that keeps the spread fixed: dividing by n squeezes it to zero, not dividing lets it grow without bound. In the Bernoulli model Sn/√n still drifts away like p√n, so there the CLT has to be stated for the centred version in the last row. In the Rademacher model the two coincide, which is why it is the natural place to study the CLT.

Simulation setup

Simulating…

Every attacker is simulated as one complete trajectory of nmax steps, and Sn is recorded at the checkpoints n = 10, 20, 50, …. The distributions at different n therefore come from the same trajectories observed at different stages of the campaign, just as a defender would see them. Random numbers come from a seeded sfc32 generator, so a given seed always reproduces the same figures. A Bernoulli step compares a 32-bit random integer with ⌊p·2³²⌋, and a Rademacher step uses a single bit of a 32-bit random word.

How large should m be? The number of attackers sets the Monte Carlo precision, not the phenomenon. With m attackers the standard error of the sample mean of Sn/n is σ/√(nm), a sample variance has a relative error of about √(2/(m − 1)) (here –), and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance between the empirical distribution and the true one is about 0.87/√m (here –) even when the model is exactly right. The last number matters for the CLT: the true distance between Sn/√n and N(0, 1) shrinks like 0.40/√n, so it can only be measured while it stays above the Monte Carlo floor, roughly for n ≲ m/5 (here n ≲ –). Larger n needs larger m.

Bernoulli. The total score grows along the line pk: every attacker keeps compromising systems at rate p, and the spread around that line, of order √k, becomes small compared with the drift. The average score starts anywhere in [0, 1] and is squeezed onto p as k grows, which is the Law of Large Numbers seen along each path. The normalized score drifts upward like p√k, so Sn/√n has no limit unless the score is centred first.

Rademacher. Without drift the total score wanders above and below zero and its typical size grows like √k: after 10,000 steps a typical attacker is about ±100 away from zero, and a few are two or three times further. The average score still collapses onto 0, and the normalized score stays in a band between about −2 and 2 at every k. The individual paths of Sk/√k never settle down, though: by the law of the iterated logarithm they keep making excursions of size about √(2 ln ln k). The CLT is a statement about the distribution at a fixed n, not about each path converging.

Reading down a column, the same sample is shown at three scales. The total score spreads out: its standard deviation grows like √n, so it is ten times larger at n = 1000 than at n = 10. The average score collapses onto μ, its standard deviation shrinking like 1/√n. The normalized score keeps the same width at every n. In the Rademacher model its histogram also takes the bell shape of N(0, 1). In the Bernoulli model the bell shape appears as well, but the whole histogram slides to the right like p√n, which is why the standardized score is shown in an extra row. At small n the histograms are visibly discrete (Sn takes only n + 1 values) and, for p ≠ 1/2, skewed; the normal curve is a poor fit there and improves as n grows.

The ratios scatter around 1 by about the predicted ±–. Dividing by n removes the fluctuations, not dividing lets them grow without bound, and dividing by √n is the exact balance between the two. That balance is what makes a non-degenerate limit, the normal distribution, possible.

Law of Large Numbers: Sn/n → μ in probability

Convergence in probability means that for every ε > 0, P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) → 0 as n → ∞. The probability is estimated as the fraction of attackers whose average score is more than ε away from μ, and compared with two upper bounds: Chebyshev's inequality, σ²/(nε²), which uses only the variance, and Hoeffding's inequality, 2 exp(−2nε²/(b − a)²), which uses the fact that the scores lie in a bounded interval [a, b].

Both estimated probabilities fall to zero, as the LLN requires. Chebyshev's bound decays only like 1/n and is very loose; Hoeffding's decays exponentially and follows the shape of the empirical curve without being exact. The actual probability is close to 2(1 − Φ(ε√n/σ)), the CLT approximation of the two tails. The Rademacher curve lies above the Bernoulli one because its steps are noisier, σ = 1 against σ = √(p(1 − p)) ≤ 1/2: the average of a noisier attacker needs more systems before it settles. Once the true probability drops below about 1/m the estimate reads 0, because rarer events are unlikely to show up among m attackers at all; this is another reason to choose m according to the precision needed.

Central Limit Theorem: Sn/√n → N(0, 1)

In the Rademacher case μ = 0 and σ = 1, so the CLT says that Sn/√n converges in distribution to the standard normal: P(Sn/√n ≤ x) → Φ(x) for every x. The histogram takes the normal shape quickly, and by n = 100 the two distribution functions are hard to tell apart by eye. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance is more precise. Sn/√n lives on a lattice of spacing 2/√n, and its distribution function jumps at each lattice point, by at most P(Sn = 0) ≈ √(2/(πn)). A continuous Φ cannot follow a jump, so the exact distance is about half of the largest one, 1/√(2πn) ≈ 0.40/√n (solid line, computed exactly from the binomial distribution). This is the 1/√n rate of the Berry–Esseen theorem, whose bound 0.4748 · E|X − μ|³/(σ³√n) (dashed) is not far above: lattice distributions like this one are the reason the constant in the theorem cannot be made much smaller. The simulated points follow the exact line until they reach the Monte Carlo floor 0.87/√m (dotted); beyond that they measure the finite number of attackers rather than the distance to the normal.

In the Bernoulli model with p ≠ 1/2 the standardized score converges too, but it starts further away: its skewness (1 − 2p)/√(np(1 − p)) is not zero, so for small n the histogram leans to one side and the distance is larger than in the symmetric case. For the Rademacher walk the skewness is exactly 0 and the excess kurtosis is −2/n, so discreteness is the only real obstacle; with m attackers these two moments are only resolved to about ±√(6/m) and ±√(24/m), which is why the table shows them clearly only for small n.

Conclusions

The three quantities are the same sum seen at three scales, and the scale decides what survives as n grows.

Bernoulli(p)Rademacher
Sngrows like np, spread √(np(1 − p))mean 0, spread √n
Sn/n→ p in probability, spread √(p(1 − p)/n)→ 0 in probability, spread 1/√n
Sn/√ndrifts like p√n; the standardized version → N(0, 1)→ N(0, 1) in distribution, at rate about 0.40/√n

For the n systems and m attackers this means the following. The average penetration rate of an attacker on a large infrastructure is predictable: it is the per-system success probability, and the LLN pins it down with an error of order 1/√n. The total damage is not predictable in the same sense: its uncertainty grows like √n, even when the average is known exactly. In the symmetric case, where gains and setbacks balance, there is no drift at all, yet a typical attacker still ends about √n away from where it started, with a normal spread that the CLT describes accurately once n is a few dozen.

The number of attackers plays two separate roles. As the sample size of the experiment it controls how precisely each of these laws can be observed, with errors of order 1/√m, which is why m has to grow with n to see the 1/√n convergence of the CLT. As a population of adversaries it sets the defender's risk: the best single attacker improves only like √(2 ln m) standard deviations, the team's pooled average becomes more predictable like 1/√(mn), while the share of systems hit by at least one attacker approaches 1 exponentially fast in m.
